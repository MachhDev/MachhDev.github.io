Comparative education

One Curriculum,
Different Classrooms

A national curriculum promises consistency. Students who have moved among French international schools and between French, American and Uruguayan systems describe something more complicated.

By Maya Chelle Education systems · 22-minute read

A curriculum decides a student’s future. Before their kids can read, parents begin the scramble to find the right school. They tour campuses, enter selective lotteries, move to better-funded school districts, and pay for private education, all for the promise of a brighter future for their children. At a higher level, lobbyists and politicians fight over school funding and resource allocation. Educational researchers analyze performance data, non-profit groups issue curriculum recommendations, and policymakers pass the final laws that shape the classroom.

Academic Researchers and Think Tanks often compare curriculums across states and countries.

The kids don’t get much of a say, but their experiences are reflected in the data gathered by these groups.

Alternative systems can completely differ from this standardized norm by placing more authority directly into the hands of the students. Approaches like Montessori, Reggio Emilia, and Waldorf believe that children are inherently capable, active drivers of their own learning

Other alternative systems include international schools,

In Montgomery County, where I grew up, there are 397 total schools. 211 public schools, which comprise 136 elementary schools, 40 middle schools, 25 high schools, 5 special schools, 2 early childhood centers, and 3 alternative education facilities, and 186 private schools, which include approximately 35 Montessori programs, 75 religiously affiliated schools, 2 boarding schools…and 2 international schools: the German International School and the French International School.

These curricula, crafted roughly 4000 miles away (6437km), are nestled into distinct, hidden corners of Maryland.

An international school is an independent private institution that provides an education using a non-local curriculum. This can be a global curriculum- such as the International Baccalaureate- or a foreign national curriculum- such as French or German systems. These have branches worldwide, allowing students to continue their education with the same curriculum when they move countries.

The French International School, also known as Lycée Rochambeau boasts over 80 represented nationalities. While France and the USA lead the student body, the community spans the globe, from larger Canada, Germany, and Spain to smaller Lebanon, Benin, and Ecuador.

Most students at Lycée Rochambeau have lived in multiple countries, experiencing various diverse cultures before turning 18.

These students follow their parents across the globe. Though their curriculum stays the same, the education they receive changes drastically.

Others are less attached to the French system. Many students have attended nearby schools in Montgomery County - Winston Churchill HS, Walter Johnson HS, and BCC to name a few- before settling on Rochambeau.

This pattern exists across the International School System. Each location hosts a mix of what can be called ‘international students’ and ‘local students.’

Thus, International Schools represent a distinctive and understudied setting for comparative education research. By concentrating students and teachers experienced with multiple curricula, they allow differences in content, pedagogy and assessment to be examined within a shared institutional environment.

Studying them allows rare insight into how education systems work. Their students bring firsthand experience of different curricula into the same classrooms, making it possible to compare what those systems teach, what they leave out and how their methods shape learning.

01

International school versus international school

One French Curriculum, Different International Schools

The official program travels. Staffing, money and course availability do not.

Curriculum

The Specialty Menu

At the Lycée Pierre Mendès-France in Tunis, Sarah, a 10th grader, studied programming. When she moved to Rochambeau, the French International School in Maryland, she remained in the French system. She did not receive the same education.

The Tunis school offered Numérique et sciences informatiques, known as NSI, as a full academic specialty. French students choose three specialty tracks for 11th grade and retain two for their final year. Because Rochambeau does not include NSI among its seven French-baccalaureate specialties, Sarah was forced to drop it.

“In Tunis, computing was a subject you could specialize in. In Maryland, the technology class was pretty outdated, ended in 10th grade, and was not offered as a specialty. That is a big difference if it is the field you want to pursue.”

Sarah, 10th grade (translated from French)
Read the original French

[« À Tunis, on avait spé techno. Ici, le cours d’informatique est hyper dépassé, s’arrête en seconde et n’est pas proposé comme spécialité. Ce n’est pas super si l’on veut faire ça comme métier. »]

Both schools teach the French national curriculum. Both prepare students for French examinations and French universities. Yet one offers a route into programming and engineering that the other does not.

This is not a small administrative difference. A student cannot choose a specialty that the school does not offer, regardless of whether it exists in the national curriculum. Those choices shape final examinations, university preparation and, potentially, a career.

A curricular option becomes an academic path.

Resources

The Budget Behind the Curriculum

National systems set programs, standards and examinations. Individual schools allocate budgets, hire teachers and decide which courses they can sustain.

“A national program can authorize a course. But you still need the budget, a qualified teacher, equipment, a computer lab, and space in the timetable. Anyways, it isn’t so easy.”

Houssem Salem, computer-science teacher (translated from French)
Read the original French

[« Un programme national autorise des cours. Mais après, il faut avoir le budget, un professeur qualifié, les outils, une salle informatique et une place dans l’emploi du temps. Bref, ce n’est pas si facile que ça. »]

A school’s budget affects which teachers it can recruit, which in turn determines which specialties it can offer. The difference between the official curriculum and the available curriculum can decide a student’s future.

National program→School budget→Teacher recruitment→Student choice
Staffing

Teachers on Three-Year Clocks

Course offerings are only one source of variation. Staffing is another.

Sarah remembered frequent staff changes in Tunis. So did Marie in Maryland and Martín in Montevideo. Some of that turnover arises from the structure of the French overseas network. The Agency for French Education Abroad recruits certain teachers on three-year contracts, renewable once. Schools also employ teachers on local contracts who may remain much longer, so not everyone rotates on the same timetable.

Still, a three-year cycle affects students.

“Each teacher completely reorganized the course dynamic. It’s O.K., but it would be nice not to have to relearn how each teacher functions and what they prefer. In France, at a public school, I knew most teachers and kids since First grade.”

Marie, student in Maryland (translated from French)
Read the original French

[« Chaque professeur réorganise la dynamique totale du cours. Ça va, mais ça serait pas mal de ne pas devoir réapprendre à chaque fois comment le prof fonctionne, ce qu’il préfère… En France, je connaissais la plupart des professeurs et des élèves depuis la grande section. »]

The movement has advantages. Teachers arriving from France may bring recent experience with national reforms, examinations and classroom practices. Rotation can prevent an international school from becoming detached from changes in France.

It can also break curricular continuity. One teacher emphasizes projects; the next emphasizes theory. One builds on the previous grade’s approach; another assumes students were taught differently.

“The rotation keeps the school alive. But the handoff is never perfect. The curriculum is the same, but the class, the students, they remember different material. They’ve all learned something different.”

Cristell Dufour, French teacher (translated from French)
Read the original French

[« La rotation maintient l’esprit de l’école. Mais c’est jamais parfait. Le programme est le même, mais chaque classe, chaque élève, ils se rappellent d’autres choses. Ils ont tous appris de façon différente. »]

A nationally standardized program does not eliminate those issues.

Continuity

The Value of Staying

Students at Rochambeau spoke fondly of teachers who had remained for long periods. That stability made it easier for departments to know what earlier classes had covered and to build a local sequence over time.

“I know how a concept was taught two years ago, so I know what I can assume and what I need to revisit. I either have my students for many years, or I collaborate with the other teachers to keep continuity.”

Florent Esteves, mathematics teacher (translated from French)
Read the original French

[« Je sais comment une notion a été enseignée en 5e et en 3e, donc je sais ce que je peux considérer comme acquis et ce qu’il faut repasser. Sans cette continuité, la révision devient une supposition. Quelques élèves sont dans ma classe avec moi pendant plusieurs années. Sinon, on parle entre les autres professeurs pour maintenir le même programme. »]

Long tenure carries its own risk. A stable department can preserve methods because they work. It can also preserve them because they are familiar. This causes complacency.

International schools therefore operate in a contradiction. Their value rests largely on consistency: a student should be able to leave Tunis, Montevideo, Madrid or Paris and continue the French program in Maryland. Yet every school differs.

“The French curriculum gives families continuity, but continuity does not mean uniformity. A school in Maryland must respond to its students, its teachers and the country around it. Our role is to preserve the framework while making it work here.”

Catherine Lévy, proviseure of Lycée Rochambeau (fictional composite)
Rotation keeps a school current.But continuity lets it build.
02

International school versus host-country school

French Structure, American Choice

The second comparison is not between campuses. It is between different ideas about how knowledge should accumulate.

The second comparison lies between the French system and the national system surrounding it.

At Rochambeau, that means comparing the French curriculum with American public education. In Montevideo, it means comparing the Lycée Français Jules-Supervielle with Uruguayan schools.

The differences appear first in how subjects are organized.

Mathematics & science

Repetition Versus Progression

“We studied probabilities last year, and the year before, and the one before that. I can’t remember a year when it wasn’t on the curriculum. The refresher can be nice, but by the time we learn something new, I’m already pretty zoned out.”

Marie, 10th grade

At her previous American school, Marie took Algebra 2. Her former classmates would move on to Probability and Statistics, perhaps as an Advanced Placement course.

“Each math topic is more compressed into clear chapters, you know?”

The French system teaches mathematics differently. Rather than separating the subject into courses such as Algebra 1, Geometry or Calculus, it returns each year to recurring fields: probability, functions, geometry, algorithms and statistics. Students work through the chapters of their manuel de mathématiques, encountering familiar material at increasing levels of complexity.

The pattern extends across subjects. Students said the French Revolution regularly resurfaced in history, urbanization in geography and DNA in Sciences de la Vie et de la Terre, or Life and Earth Sciences.

The principle is sensible. A student of 16 should not study a subject in the same way as a student of 13. Revisiting material strengthens retention and allows teachers to replace a simplified explanation with a more accurate or in-depth one.

“The repetition is intentional,” Laurent, the mathematics teacher, said. “A probability problem at 16 is supposed to demand more than a probability problem at 13. The difficulty is knowing when review has stopped being useful.”

The problem is time.

“You often spend the beginning of the year waiting for the new part,” said Ismael, 16, who attended school in France before moving to Maryland. “The teacher repeats what everyone is supposed to know. By the time we reach the new part, the difficult content is rushed.”

Students who understood the earlier material considered the repetition wasteful. Students who had missed it often appreciated the review. Yet even they sometimes found it too compressed to repair the gap. The course can therefore move too slowly for one group and too quickly for another.

The pattern also produces an uneven kind of expertise.

Several Life and Earth Science students said they had an unusually detailed knowledge of the immune system but could not recall studying the nervous, circulatory or respiratory systems at comparable depth.

Aruni described drawing detailed renditions of the immune system, using 3-D modeling software, making a poster board, creating a brief documentary-style video and taking dozens of quizzes on the topic. When asked, she could summarize the system’s full operation using advanced vocabulary.

The depth was impressive. So was the imbalance.

French science also joins disciplines that American schools often separate. Biology and geology are taught together, as are chemistry and physics. Where many American students take one dedicated science course each year, French students move among recurring themes such as “The Living Planet” and “The Human Body and Health.”

The French approach returns to concepts across several years. The American approach more often completes one disciplinary course before moving to the next. One risks repetition. The other risks fragmentation.

French sequence Return, deepen, repeat.
  • Stronger long-term reinforcement
  • More depth inside recurring themes
  • Risk: review consumes new material
American sequence Complete, advance, specialize.
  • Clearer course-by-course progression
  • More room to redirect a path
  • Risk: completed subjects disappear
History

History by Theme, History by Timeline

“It would be nice to have a more linear idea of history,” Fatma said. “In class, we’re often given three or four primary or secondary accounts. Pictures, speech excerpts, journal passages, that kind of thing. They center around a theme. Then they ask us a question, and we have to answer using the evidence. We learned a clear méthodologie for how to structure paragraph answers.”

Josephine encountered something different at Bethesda-Chevy Chase High School.

“When we learned the Revolutionary War, it was a lot of dates,” she said. “Small battles and details. It kind of makes you lose track of the big picture. And there was a lot of multiple-choice stuff too.”

A Rochambeau teacher who also teaches United States history offered a correction.

“When I teach my AP history classes, I always ask, ‘So what?’” he said. “Chronology is everything. You don’t have to know exact dates. Just remember the sequence.”

Officially, the French secondary program retains a chronology. Rochambeau describes 10th-grade history as extending from antiquity and the Middle Ages to the 18th century, with later grades covering 1789 to the present.

Students did not always experience it that way. They remembered themes: political power, empire, industrialization, cities, war, decolonization and democracy. Within those units, teachers worked heavily with speeches, maps, photographs, statistics and extracts from primary sources.

A subject might return in a later grade from a different angle. Students could know one part of it extremely well while retaining a surprisingly weak overall chronology.

“I could analyze a document about colonial rule,” said Alex, who had attended a French school in Spain. “But if you gave me 10 events and asked me to put them in order, I was less certain than I should have been.”

His American history class followed a clearer line from colonization and independence through the Civil War, industrialization, the world wars and the modern period. It went deeply into American battles, presidents, constitutional amendments and court decisions.

“I always knew what came next,” Alex said. “But a lot of studying meant remembering which battle, which year, which amendment.”

Every national curriculum appeared to share one tendency: each devoted an enormous share of its history program to one country or region.

Alex knew the tactical course of the American Civil War in detail but could not identify many of the countries composing Africa, Latin America or Asia.

“Constantinople fell while the Inca Empire was expanding, Tenochtitlan was flourishing and Ming China ruled from the Forbidden City. We call it ‘the end of the Middle Ages,’ but only Europe was in the Middle Ages.”

Alex, after studying in French and American systems
Classroom method

Analysis Versus Recall

Many Rochambeau students appreciated that their system strengthened and tested critical thinking rather than memorization. They also emphasized that nearly every test required written answers. Whether a student knew the material appeared differently on paper than it did in multiple-choice questions.

The same students could analyze a literary passage in remarkable detail. They identified shifts in tense, explained the effect of a metaphor and constructed arguments from small textual clues. Final examinations require skills such as presenting a detailed oral analysis of a short poem or writing at length about a thesis drawn from a novel.

French classes teach students how a subject expects them to think. Literary commentary, historical document study, philosophical dissertation and mathematical demonstration are analytical methods students practice repeatedly until the structure becomes second nature.

The advantage is learning to draw conclusions from evidence and explain a response.

The weakness is that a method can become a standard formula. A student may learn the architecture of an essay without developing a particularly original argument. Teachers may spend weeks preparing students to satisfy an examination because the national examination gives that formula unusual power.

“The structure helps when you have something to say,” Camille said. “Sometimes it also hides that you do not have much to say.”

Josephine saw a similar weakness in American testing.

“I wasn’t a fan of multiple choice,” she said. “When I knew the answer, sometimes none of the choices really fit what I wanted to say. When I didn’t know, I guessed. Sometimes I was right. Teachers also focused a lot on training us for SATs and other standardized tests. Honestly, neither system is perfect.”

A formula can train analysis.It can also imitate it.
03

A second host-country comparison

The View From Montevideo

In Uruguay, the contrast is not mainly between analysis and recall. It is between disciplinary depth and cross-disciplinary application.

Martín lives in Montevideo, Uruguay. He attended a British kindergarten and then a Uruguayan public school. In seventh grade, he transferred to the Lycée Français Jules-Supervielle.

“The French school obviously receives more funding, so we have more resources and nicer classrooms. Having such international teachers is really interesting; it adds a lot of variety to the school. Honestly, for me, the public-school curriculum is pretty similar. We have longer hours, though.”

Martín, student in Montevideo (translated from Spanish)
Read the original Spanish

[« El colegio francés obviamente recibe más fondos, así que tenemos más recursos, mejores salones… Tener profesores tan internacionales es interesantísimo; le agrega mucha variedad al colegio. Sinceramente, para mí el programa de la escuela pública es bastante parecido. Pero acá tenemos jornadas más largas. »]

Students in Montevideo follow the French program while also studying Uruguayan material and learning a third language. Preparing for the French baccalaureate adds to the course load.

Uruguay’s national curriculum is organized around 10 broad competencies, including communication, critical thinking, scientific reasoning and the ability to apply knowledge. The French system more clearly defines the content and analytical forms expected within each subject.

“At the French school, the subject has harder borders,” Martín said. “You go further inside it. In the Uruguayan school, teachers sometimes connected subjects better, but sometimes none of the subjects went very far.”

A strong interdisciplinary project can force students to select information, apply several forms of knowledge and solve a less scripted problem. A weak one can place several lightly treated subjects beneath the same broad theme.

“The competency model asks students to use what they know. But if the content is thin, the project will be thin too.”

Lucía Fernández, public-school teacher in Montevideo (translated from Spanish)
Read the original Spanish

[« El modelo por competencias les pide que usen lo que aprendieron. Pero si el contenido es superficial, el proyecto también lo es. »]

The French approach offers clearer progression within each discipline. The Uruguayan approach aims to connect learning across disciplines. In practice, Martín said, the success of either system depends heavily on the teacher.

French model Go further inside the subject.

Clear disciplines and demanding subject-specific methods.

Uruguayan model Connect knowledge across subjects.

Broad competencies tested through application.

Shared risk The teacher decides whether it works.

Structure cannot guarantee depth or connection.

04

Examinations shape instruction

What the Assessments Reward

A final exam does more than measure a curriculum. It reaches backward and changes how the curriculum is taught.

The French baccalaureate exerts pressure backward through the curriculum.

Under the current structure, final examinations account for 60 percent of the final result and continuous assessment for 40 percent. Students take national examinations in French, philosophy, their chosen specialties and the Grand oral.

60%Final examinations
40%Continuous assessment

The arrangement gives the curriculum a common goal. A student in Bethesda, Tunis or France prepares for recognizable tasks evaluated against national expectations.

It also encourages repetition. If the final examination requires a specific analytical form, schools have reason to rehearse that form for years. Material may return because the examination assumes mastery. Teachers may spend time on work students already understand because the cost of overlooking a weakness is high.

Josephine considered the final examinations more demanding than the course-credit system she encountered in Maryland.

The American system is more modular. Students combine required courses with Advanced Placement subjects, career programs, arts, computer science and other electives. They receive repeated feedback and can adjust their path. That grants them more control over their education and more closely resembles the American university system.

But course choice can also create gaps. Completing a course may close a subject rather than establish material that will be tested again years later. Grades from different teachers and classes appear on the same transcript even when the work differs sharply in depth.

French systemBetter at telling students that knowledge remains in force.

American systemBetter at allowing students to redirect their education.

One can waste time through repetition. The other can create gaps through choice.

“I’m not really sure where I’m going to university. I have been in this school all my life. But it didn’t prepare me for U.S. university at all. I don’t even really know the system. I’m being trained for the French system, but I don’t know anyone in France. I might go to Canada, honestly.”

Maya, 11th grade
05

No clean winner

Which System Is More Effective?

Each system solves a problem the others expose and creates another in the process.

The interviews point to a limited conclusion.

French education is strong at cumulative disciplinary training. It teaches students to analyze difficult texts, construct formal arguments and work toward a common external standard. Its repeated return to the same material can produce unusual depth.

It can also consume years revisiting familiar ground. Students may become highly trained in a narrow set of intellectual forms while receiving little exposure to fields their particular school does not offer. Its promise of national uniformity is weakened by differences in specialty menus, teacher recruitment and local requirements.

American public education offers more course choice and makes room for technology, advanced classes, practical subjects and changes in direction. Its history courses may give students a clearer chronology and firmer command of facts.

Content depends more heavily on the district, school and teacher. Students can complete impressive collections of courses without encountering a coherent sequence. The attention paid to grades, credits and individual assignments can turn education into transcript management.

Uruguay states its ambitions in the language of competencies and application. Its curriculum asks students to use knowledge, connect subjects and solve less scripted problems. Done well, that corrects the tendency of disciplinary systems to confuse coverage with understanding.

Done poorly, competency-based education produces broad projects without enough knowledge underneath them.

France

Depth and common standards

Strength: cumulative analytical training.

Cost: repetition and narrow specialization.

United States

Choice and redirection

Strength: modular courses and flexibility.

Cost: gaps and uneven sequences.

Uruguay

Connection and application

Strength: cross-disciplinary competencies.

Cost: breadth without enough content.

Interestingly, students within the International school system don’t describe themselves as ahead or behind. While less-than-satisfactory grades and last-minute projects abound across systems and countries, every student seems to possess a broader understanding of life as non-linear. Varied experiences within school and beyond classroom walls shows that every path is subjective, man-made.

Each curriculum possesses strengths and gaps. Beyond this, there is a distance between the curriculum a nation standardizes and the education an individual school actually provides. Ultimately, examining the differences offers insight to improve each.

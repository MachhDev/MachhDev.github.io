By Maya Chelle Beirut port explosion · August 4, 2020 Smoke rises from the Port of Beirut before the main explosion. The blast wave reaches the camera. BEIRUT, Lebanon — On summer weekends, residents leave Beirut for the beaches north of the city or vacation in the mountains, where the air cools and restaurants look down toward the Mediterranean. At night, traffic builds outside the bars and clubs of Gemmayzeh and Mar Mikhael. Music carries from rooftops. Parties fill hotel terraces and village squares.
Beirut sits between the Mediterranean and the mountains, a city where seaside promenades, Ottoman and French Mandate-era buildings, and modern glass towers occupy the same skyline. On summer weekends, residents swim along the coast in the morning and drive into the Mount Lebanon range by evening, where villages overlook cedar-covered slopes and the sea beyond. The city's restaurants, music venues and bars have long drawn visitors from across the region. In districts such as Gemmayzeh and Mar Mikhael, century-old stone buildings house cafés, galleries and rooftop terraces that stay crowded late into the night. Beirut's appeal has always rested on contrasts: mountains and coastline, tradition and modernity, affluence and hardship, all compressed into a narrow strip of land along the eastern Mediterranean. Videos of wedding parties dancing into the night as missiles light the skies in a dangerous display have gone viral. These scenes endure through wars, blackouts and political crises. They form the spirit of the Lebanese people: social, spirited, and determined to continue their lives, even amidst chaos. Lebanon stands apart from the region in religious tolerance: Maronite Christians, Sunni Muslims, Shiite Muslims, and Druze share the city. Yet their history has not been peaceful. Civil war broke out in 1975 and lasted 15 years. Syrian troops entered in 1976 and remained until 2005. Israel invaded in 1978 and again in 1982, then occupied part of southern Lebanon until 2000.
Lebanon has always played a role as the region's battleground. To offer a simplified version, for those who don’t follow the complex history of the Middle East: Iran backs Hezbollah, Saudi Arabia supports Sunni political figures, Syria exerts significant influence, and Israel fights a war against Hezbollah.
Understanding the country's overlapping political, sectarian and regional rivalries is never straightforward.

Yet the spirit of the people has endured, frequently driving out invading militaries and unsuitable regimes through mass protests. Still, political order has never recovered. Militias enter Parliament, Syrian intelligence shapes government decisions, and assassinations via car bombs have historically been the preferred way to eliminate political opposition.

Often, instability and chaos rule.
The government has repeatedly stopped functioning. Rival blocs left the presidency vacant from 2014 to 2016. Another presidential vacuum began in 2022 and lasted until 2025.
For many, this kind of governmental shutdown is inconceivable. Despite their resilience, all of this political instability has, of course, greatly affected citizens' daily lives. Throughout history, particularly during military invasions, electricity failures have been commonplace. A garbage crisis has left refuse piled in the streets for weeks. Public debt rises, and infrastructure deteriorates. Bombs and war have often left entire blocks in shambles. Still, Lebanon rebuilds homes, restaurants, shopping districts…life goes on.
In October 2019, protesters gathered across the country. The political chaos has become unbearable, and they demand a change in leadership. They demand order and transparency. They blame the entire political class for corruption and economic decline. Banks soon restrict withdrawals, the currency collapses, and families lose access to savings. Millions of Lebanese Pounds and US Dollars locked in people's accounts, inaccessible. Then Covid-19 arrives. Despite the political pandemonium, the health sector must fight the pandemic. Importing medicine and retaining staff become nearly impossible. The pandemic strained resources and morale across the globe. In Lebanon, it could not have come at a worse time.
The response can look carefree but comes with constant vigilance. Residents notice aircraft overhead, listen to distant explosions, and know how quickly a celebration can turn.
Which is why, when a mysterious explosion shook the capital, theories ran wild.
In the first minutes after the blast, social media filled faster than official information. Videos circulated alongside claims that Israel had struck a Hezbollah weapons depot, that military aircraft had been seen overhead, or that an ammunition warehouse had detonated. None of those explanations had been verified.
"I felt like I'm dying, I still can't believe it," one resident wrote on social media as footage of the explosion spread online. Similar messages appeared by the thousands as residents attempted to understand what had happened to their city. 【1-218df9】
On Aug. 4, 2020, an explosion shatters the city. Its magnitude shakes the ground up to 150 miles (250km) away.
It starts as a fire. From a distance, a small orange blur with a rising trail of smoke.
The cloud of smoke grows above the grain silos near Hangar 12, a warehouse beside the water. Small explosions crack inside the building.
People step onto balconies and rooftops to record it. One video begins with a gray plume over the port, the camera steady as the smoke thickens. Before spectators can process what has occurred, an orange flash appears at the base of the cloud. A thick white pressure cloud expands across the waterfront. It reaches the camera within seconds. The sonar shockwave follows, knocking the cameraman flat. The video ends with screams.
The explosion occurs at about 6:08 p.m. Its pressure wave moves through Beirut, tearing doors from their frames and turning windows into flying blades. It cuts through homes, offices, schools and hospitals. Later counts place the death toll at no fewer than 236. Over 7,000 people suffered injuries. The blast forced an estimated 300,000 residents from their homes and damaged tens of thousands of apartments. How to explain such a catastrophic explosion? Such senseless pain and suffering?

Before facts emerge, residents searched for an explanation. First-person accounts from residents on the ground span across various theories. Many assure that an aircraft flew above the port. Confidently, they affirm that they saw something fly overhead. Some even describe a faint whistle, the kind a falling bomb might make. Messages accuse Israel of striking a Hezbollah weapons store or Syria of sabotaging Lebanon while its government is weak.
Other residents suspect arson. They point to the initial fire and ask who started it. Some claim the sparks within the initial have been minor explosives set off throughout the hangar. The speed at which these serious accusations appear is frightening. It is indicative of the consequences for a people plagued by conflict.

In the days after the blast, volunteers sweep glass and remove debris. Restaurants turn their kitchens into relief centers. Residents offer rooms, medicine and transportation. The people support eachother and work hard to recover, but anger runs deep.
French President Emmanuel Macron visited Lebanon after the blast. As he walks through damaged Beirut streets two days after the explosion, residents surround him, denounce Lebanon’s leaders and ask France to intervene.
Some plead with him not to trust the political class. "Don't give money to our government," one resident tells him as the crowd presses around the French president. 【2-ebde44】
Elsewhere in the crowd, residents chant "Revolution!" and "The people want to bring down the regime!" The slogans echo demonstrations that had already swept the country before the explosion and reveal how quickly anger over the blast becomes anger at Lebanon's political establishment. 【3-c191c9】【4-cad89d】
An online petition calls for Lebanon to return to a French mandate for 10 years. More than 60,000 people sign it. Some appeals end with “Vive la France.”
The petition proposes reversing the independence process that Lebanese leaders begin in 1943 and France advances through the transfer of administrative powers in January 1944. Many Lebanese sign because they no longer believe their own government can protect them or manage the country.
The petition shows how far public confidence falls after the explosion.
Lebanon’s government resigns six days after the blast. Yet accountability is scarce. Officials refuse questioning, invoke immunity and challenge the judges who summon them. Judge Tarek Bitar resumes the inquiry in 2025 after another two-year interruption. Families of the dead return to the port with photographs of their relatives. Behind them, the damaged grain silos stand above the Mediterranean. They face the same question that follows Lebanon through every war and political crisis: whether anyone with power will answer for what happens to those without.

The Explosion That Shook the Mediterranean

Lebanon has endured civil war, occupation, assassinations, regional pressures, and political instability. When a blast tore through the city, many residents instinctively assumed it was another attack. The explanation was, in some ways, more disturbing: the danger had been sitting in plain sight.

By Maya Chelle Beirut port explosion · July 8, 2021

BEIRUT, Lebanon — On summer weekends, residents leave Beirut for the beaches north of the city or climb into the mountains, where the air cools and restaurants look down toward the Mediterranean.

At night, traffic builds outside the bars and clubs of Gemmayzeh and Mar Mikhael. Music carries from rooftops. Parties fill hotel terraces and village squares.
The city is vibrant in natural resources, culture, and nightlife. Videos of wedding parties dancing into the night as missiles light the skies in a dangerous display have gone viral. These scenes endure through wars, blackouts and political crises. They form the spirit of the Lebanese people: social, spirited, and determined to continue their lives, even amidst chaos. Lebanon stands apart from the region in religious tolerance: Maronite Christians, Sunni Muslims, Shiite Muslims, and Druze share the city. Yet their history has not been peaceful. Civil war broke out in 1975 and lasted 15 years. Syrian troops entered in 1976 and remained until 2005. Israel invaded in 1978 and again in 1982, then occupied part of southern Lebanon until 2000.
Lebanon has always played a role as the region's battleground. To offer a simplified version, for those who don’t follow the complex history of the Middle East: Iran backs Hezbollah, Saudi Arabia supports Sunni political figures, Syria exerts significant influence, and Israel fights a war against Hezbollah.
It can make your head spin.

Yet the spirit of the people has endured, frequently driving out invading militaries and unsuitable regimes through mass protests. Still, political order has never recovered. Militias enter Parliament, Syrian intelligence shapes government decisions, and assassinations via car bombs have historically been the preferred way to eliminate political opposition.

Often, instability and chaos rule.
The government has repeatedly stopped functioning. Rival blocs left the presidency vacant from 2014 to 2016. Another presidential vacuum began in 2022 and lasted until 2025.
For many, this kind of governmental shutdown is inconceivable. Despite their resilience, all of this political instability has, of course, greatly affected citizens' daily lives. Throughout history, particularly during military invasions, electricity failures have been commonplace. A garbage crisis has left refuse piled in the streets for weeks. Public debt rises, and infrastructure deteriorates. Bombs and war have often left entire blocks in shambles. Still, Lebanon rebuilds homes, restaurants, shopping districts…life goes on.
In October 2019, protesters gathered across the country. The political chaos has become unbearable, and they demand a change in leadership. They demand order and transparency. They blame the entire political class for corruption and economic decline. Banks soon restrict withdrawals, the currency collapses, and families lose access to savings. Millions of Lebanese Pounds and US Dollars locked in people's accounts, inaccessible. Then Covid-19 arrives. Despite the political pandemonium, the health sector must fight the pandemic. Importing medicine and retaining staff become nearly impossible. The pandemic strained resources and morale across the globe. In Lebanon, it could not have come at a worse time.
The response can look carefree but comes with constant vigilance. Residents notice aircraft overhead, listen to distant impacts, and know how quickly a celebration can turn.
Which is why, when a mysterious explosion shook the capital, theories ran wild.
On Aug. 4, 2020, an explosion shatters the city. Its magnitude shakes the ground up to 150 miles (250km) away.
It starts as a fire. From a distance, a small orange blur with a rising trail of smoke.
The cloud of smoke grows above the grain silos near Hangar 12, a warehouse beside the water. Small explosions crack inside the building.
People step onto balconies and rooftops to record it. One video begins with a gray plume over the port, the camera steady as the smoke thickens. Before spectators can process what has occurred, an orange flash appears at the base of the cloud. A thick white pressure cloud expands across the waterfront. It reaches the camera within seconds. The sonar shockwave follows, knocking the cameraman flat. The video ends with screams.
The explosion occurs at about 6:08 p.m. Its pressure wave moves through Beirut, tearing doors from their frames and turning windows into flying blades. It cuts through homes, offices, schools and hospitals. Later counts place the death toll at no fewer than 236. Over 7,000 people suffered injuries. The blast forced an estimated 300,000 residents from their homes and damaged tens of thousands of apartments. How to explain such a catastrophic explosion? Such senseless pain and suffering?

Before facts emerge, residents searched for an explanation. First-person accounts from residents on the ground span across various theories. Many assure that an aircraft flew above the port. Confidently, they affirm that they saw something fly overhead. Some even describe a faint whistle, the kind a falling bomb might make. Messages accuse Israel of striking a Hezbollah weapons store or Syria of sabotaging Lebanon while its government is weak.
Other residents suspect arson. They point to the initial fire and ask who started it. Some claim the sparks within the initial have been minor explosives set off throughout the hangar. The speed at which these serious accusations appear is frightening. It is indicative of the consequences for a people plagued by conflict.
Lebanon’s civil aviation authorities later report that local radar detects no military aircraft during the relevant period. Investigators find no reliable evidence of an airstrike, nor of arson. The emerging explanation runs deeper: mere negligence. Inside Hangar 12, officials have stored ammonium nitrate for nearly six years. Farmers commonly use the chemical as fertilizer, but heat, contamination and confinement can make a large stockpile explode. Customs officers, port administrators, judges, security agencies and ministries had exchanged letters and emails about it. Some ask to sell the cargo. Others propose re-exporting it or handing it to the army. No agency removes it.

This form of inaction has been a decades-long trend. In 2015, a similar situation took place. The Netherlands, Great Britain, Germany, and Sierra Leone all respectively offered to take charge of Lebanon's great garbage crisis. A country drowning in waste, and fortunate to have foreign leaders take charge to alleviate the strain. There was much deliberation.
A formal deal never took place. This time, of course, inaction came at a much higher cost.
A state security report had reached President Michel Aoun and Prime Minister Hassan Diab in July 2020. It described the ammonium nitrate as dangerous and highly flammable. It was highlighted as an urgent matter. Nothing was done. Less than three weeks later, Beirut explodes. The disaster carries a bitter irony for a country accustomed to foreign attack. No army crossed the border, no pilot dropped a bomb, no espionage took place. Years of administrative delay are all it took. A betrayal from the inside. The cause of the initial fire remains unresolved, but investigators consider sparks from repair work and a fire that spread from another part of the port. The pressure wave strikes a moving car as a passenger records the port. One archive video shows the blast from inside a moving car. The person filming keeps the smoke in view through the passenger window. The shock wave strikes the vehicle and sends the phone toward the ceiling. Another video looks down toward the port through a high window. It circulates with the claim that a student records it from a dormitory and survives. The file does not identify the student or the building. It records the view from an ordinary room as the explosion moves toward it. Footage circulated online and attributed to an unidentified student in a dormitory. Firefighters reach the port while the warehouse burns. No one warns them about the ammonium nitrate. Several stand near Hangar 12 when the main explosion occurs. Across Beirut, hospitals are hit by the pressure wave, then by the wave of incoming patients. Covid-19 had already fills intensive-care beds. On the day before the blast, the head of Lebanon’s principal public hospital for coronavirus patients warned that it approaches capacity. Then, the explosion disables three hospitals and heavily damages three others. Glass and debris tear through its rooms. Staff members evacuate patients and treat people outside. Coronavirus precautions collapse. Beirut loses the equivalent of about 500 hospital beds just as thousands of injured residents seek treatment. As bleeding residents crowd entrances and sidewalks, families move from hospital to hospital searching for missing relatives. A photograph in the archive shows Pamela Zeinoun, a nurse in the neonatal intensive-care unit at Saint George, holding three premature babies against her chest. The blast knocks her unconscious. When she recovers, she removes the infants from damaged incubators and carries them out. Photojournalist Bilal Jawich photographs her holding the babies while trying to use a telephone. Four nurses at Saint George die. The shock wave enters diplomatic residences as easily as hospital rooms. Hedwig Waltmans-Molier, the 55-year-old wife of the Netherlands’ ambassador, stands beside her husband in their living room when the blast reaches them. She suffers critical injuries and dies four days later. The explosion also tore through Beirut’s cultural center. The Sursock Museum and the Sursock Palace across from it suffer extensive damage. Windows shattered, roofs torn off, and historic interiors open to the weather. The destruction spreads through Gemmayzeh, Mar Mikhael and Ashrafieh. UNESCO later reports damage to at least 8,000 buildings, including 640 historic structures. Inspectors consider about 60 at risk of collapse. A damaged historic residence in Beirut. Debris fills a residential street after the blast. The pressure wave damages roofs, windows and balconies across the city’s historic districts. Rooms stand open to the street after exterior walls collapse. Residents and volunteers move through the wreckage. A view from inside one damaged structure toward another. The archive includes a memorial for Joe Akiki, a 23-year-old electrical-engineering student who works as an electrician at the port to pay his tuition. Hours before the explosion, Mr. Akiki calls his mother and tells her he has taken an overnight shift. He sends friends a video of the port fire. Search teams recover his body several days later. The memorial claims that he called from under the rubble and that authorities blocked foreign rescuers from reaching him. In the days after the blast, volunteers sweep glass and remove debris. Restaurants turn their kitchens into relief centers. Residents offer rooms, medicine and transportation. The people support eachother and work hard to recover, but anger runs deep. French President Emmanuel Macron visited Lebanon after the blast. As he walks through damaged Beirut streets two days after the explosion, residents surround him, denounce Lebanon’s leaders and ask France to intervene. An online petition calls for Lebanon to return to a French mandate for 10 years. More than 60,000 people sign it. Some appeals end with “Vive la France.” The petition proposes reversing the independence process that Lebanese leaders begin in 1943 and France advances through the transfer of administrative powers in January 1944. Many Lebanese sign because they no longer believe their own government can protect them or manage the country. The petition shows how far public confidence falls after the explosion. Lebanon’s government resigns six days after the blast. Yet accountability is scarce. Officials refuse questioning, invoke immunity and challenge the judges who summon them. Judge Tarek Bitar resumes the inquiry in 2025 after another two-year interruption. Families of the dead return to the port with photographs of their relatives. Behind them, the damaged grain silos stand above the Mediterranean. They face the same question that follows Lebanon through every war and political crisis: whether anyone with power will answer for what happens to those without.

Reporting notes and sources

Casualty totals change as authorities and rights groups identify additional deaths. This article uses the 2025 count from Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch: at least 236 people killed and more than 7,000 injured. The original shipment contains about 2,750 tons of ammonium nitrate; investigators have not established how much remains or detonates on Aug. 4.

US Press-Freedom Monitor

01
Rule pendingEffective Sept. 15

Foreign correspondents will face fixed periods of admission

The Department of Homeland Security is ending open-ended “duration of status” admission for representatives of foreign media. A final rule published July 17 allows most journalists in I status to remain for the length of an assignment, up to 240 days. Journalists traveling on passports issued by the People’s Republic of China are generally limited to 90 days.

Extensions remain possible. The difference is that a long-term correspondent will now have to ask the government for permission to stay at regular intervals. DHS says the change allows more frequent screening and oversight. News organizations and press-freedom groups say the renewal cycle could make multi-year assignments more expensive and less predictable.

The press-freedom question is not whether foreign journalists can still enter the United States; they can. It is whether repeated immigration review gives the government leverage over reporters whose work may be sharply critical of the administration in power. Reporters Without Borders has urged Congress to step in.

WatchImplementation guidance, extension-processing times, litigation and any sign that bureaus shorten or cancel assignments because of the new timetable.
02
Proceeding openMB Docket 26-131

Eight ABC stations are in an extraordinary early license review

The Federal Communications Commission has required all eight ABC-owned television stations to seek license renewal years before they ordinarily would. The Media Bureau ordered the filings in April; ABC submitted them under protest in May. The FCC opened a public docket on May 29.

The agency says the review is tied to questions about whether Disney and ABC complied with federal law, including rules against unlawful employment discrimination, and whether the stations have served the public interest. Broadcast licensing is a longstanding FCC function. What makes this case unusual is the timing: the stations would not normally begin renewal proceedings until 2028 or later.

ABC has called the order unlawful and unconstitutional. Reuters reported that the action is the first early renewal review imposed on a major television broadcaster in more than five decades and followed repeated public calls by President Donald Trump for regulators to act against ABC. That sequence is central to the press-freedom concern: a licensing process can become coercive if editorial decisions are treated as grounds for regulatory punishment.

WatchThe Commission’s stated legal standard, reply filings in the docket and whether any final decision turns on programming or editorial choices rather than neutral licensing criteria.
03
In courtRule restored July 17

Pentagon escort restrictions remain in force while litigation continues

The fight over press access at the Pentagon has moved quickly. A federal district judge blocked the Defense Department’s escort policy on July 1, finding that the restrictions burdened newsgathering. On July 17, a federal appeals court stayed that ruling, allowing the policy to take effect again while the case proceeds.

The rules require reporters to be escorted in parts of the Pentagon where they previously moved more freely. The department has also tightened photography rules and converted its press office into a Sensitive Compartmented Information Facility, ending routine walk-in access to a space reporters had long used to reach public-affairs officials.

The dispute is about more than hallway access. Pentagon correspondents build stories through informal conversations, source development and observation outside formal briefings. Restricting those encounters can narrow what reporters learn even when scheduled press conferences continue. The Defense Department says the measures protect classified information. The New York Times and other challengers say they impede constitutionally protected reporting.

WatchThe appellate case, any changes to escort rules or credential policy and whether smaller or specialist outlets retain the same practical access as large national news organizations.
04
Indicator2026 index

The United States fell to 64th in the global press-freedom index

The United States ranks 64th of 180 countries and territories in Reporters Without Borders’ 2026 World Press Freedom Index, down seven places from 2025. RSF’s country assessment points to economic strain in the news business, political hostility toward journalists, government pressure and violence against reporters.

The group says more than 170 attacks on journalists were recorded in the United States in 2025, almost double the previous year, citing the U.S. Press Freedom Tracker.

The ranking is a measure of the wider environment, not proof about any single visa rule, lawsuit or license proceeding. Its value here is context: the federal disputes above are unfolding during a broader deterioration in the conditions under which American journalism operates.

WatchVerified attacks and arrests, source-protection disputes, newsroom closures and new federal or state restrictions on access to public information.

Why these cases belong together

None of the four developments is identical. Immigration law, broadcast licensing and access to military facilities rest on different statutes and legal doctrines. The common issue is practical independence: whether reporters can enter, remain, gather information and publish without facing discretionary government pressure tied to their work.

Method

This monitor tracks federal actions with a direct effect on reporting, newsroom operations or access to public institutions. Primary government records are used where available and checked against independent reporting and specialist press-freedom analysis. Status labels describe the procedural posture as of the update date.

Updates replace earlier status language when a court, agency or rulemaking moves.
